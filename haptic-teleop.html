Haptic Teleoperation

Current system

Overview

Haptic teleoperation lets a person operate a robotic hand from a distance while feeling what it touches. The operator's hand is tracked with an ordinary webcam and mirrored in real time by a servo-driven robotic hand, and pressure sensors on the robot's fingertips measure contact and send it back to the operator. The goal of this dissertation is to close that loop with wearable haptic devices, so the operator can both command the hand and feel its grip — improving dexterity and confidence in remote manipulation.

System flow

The system runs as a closed loop with two data paths. The feedforward path captures the operator's motion and mirrors it onto the robot; the feedback path measures contact at the robot's fingertips and returns it to the operator.

How it's designed

Four components make up the loop: hand tracking turns webcam video into robot joint angles, the ESP32 firmware drives the servos, the robotic hand performs the motion, and the fingertip sensors measure what it touches.

Hand tracking & retargeting

  • Python
  • OpenCV
  • dex-retargeting

A webcam feed is processed with OpenCV to detect hand keypoints, and dex-retargeting maps those keypoints onto the robotic hand's kinematics to produce target joint positions. This runs on the laptop and streams the joint commands over a serial (COM) port to the ESP32.

ESP32 firmware

  • C++
  • ESP32

A C++ program on the ESP32 receives the joint-position messages over the serial link and converts them into position commands for the servos, turning the retargeted motion into physical movement of the hand.

Robotic hand

  • Servos

The hand is actuated by servos — one per controlled joint — that follow the angles streamed from the ESP32, so the robot mirrors the operator's hand in real time.

Fingertip pressure sensing

  • Hall-effect sensor
  • Magnet
  • Silicone

Each fingertip carries a custom sensor: a magnet embedded in silicone sits above a Hall-effect sensor, so contact deforms the silicone and shifts the magnetic field it reads. The resulting force signals are sent back to the laptop and form the basis of the haptic feedback loop.

What's next

The control path and fingertip sensing are working; the remaining work focuses on calibration, the feedback hardware, and evaluation.

  • Normalise the fingertip sensors

    Calibrate the Hall-effect fingertip sensors so their readings are consistent and comparable across all fingers.

  • Design the haptic feedback devices

    Build wearable vibrotactile, pressure, and electrotactile devices that render the measured fingertip forces back to the operator.

  • Test with human subjects

    Evaluate the full teleoperation loop with human participants to measure how haptic feedback affects dexterity and task performance.
